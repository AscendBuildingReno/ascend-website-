How Much Does It Cost
to Plaster a Room

Plastering a standard room in 2026 costs between £400 and £850 in the North West, depending on the size of the room, the condition of what's already on the walls, and whether the ceiling is included. A skim over sound existing plaster sits at the lower end. A room that needs stripping back, overboarding and a full two-coat system sits at the top — and occasionally beyond it.

Below is what those numbers actually break down to, so you can look at a quote and tell whether it's fair.

These are Lancashire and Merseyside figures. Expect to pay 20–35% more for the same work in London and the South East, which is why national price comparison sites often look wrong when you're getting quotes in Burnley or Southport.

What actually drives the price

Room size — but not the way most people assume

Plastering is priced by surface area, not floor area. A 3m x 3m room with 2.4m ceilings has around 28m² of wall before you deduct the door and window. Add the ceiling and you're at 37m². That is the number a plasterer is working from when they price the job, and it's why a room that "looks small" can still be half a day's work for two people.

Tall Victorian ceilings — common across Burnley, Padiham and the older Southport stock — add a surprising amount. A room with 2.9m ceilings has roughly 20% more wall area than the same footprint with modern 2.4m ceilings, plus the added time of working off a hop-up or tower rather than off the floor.

Condition of the existing surface

This is the single biggest variable, and it's why quotes over the phone are close to worthless.

  • Sound existing plaster — a straight skim. Fastest, cheapest, best finish-per-pound in the business.
  • Blown or hollow plaster — needs hacking off back to brick, then a backing coat before the finish coat. Roughly doubles the labour and adds materials.
  • Artex or textured coating — either overboarded or scraped and sealed. Overboarding is usually the better call and often the cheaper one once you account for the time scraping takes.
  • Old lath and plaster ceilings — common in pre-1930s properties. If the laths are sound you can overboard. If they're not, it's a strip-out, and that's a different job with a skip in it.

Ceiling in or out

A ceiling is roughly 30–40% of the plastering in a typical room. Leaving it out saves real money — but if the ceiling is going to need doing within two or three years, doing it now while the room is already stripped and sheeted costs far less than coming back to a decorated room later.

Access, parking and protection

Third-floor flat with no lift, or a job on a terraced street with no parking bay, adds time to every material run. It's rarely a huge cost but it's a legitimate line on a quote, and a contractor who mentions it is usually one who's actually looked at the job.

Skim vs full re-plaster: which do you need?

A skim is a 2–3mm finish coat over a surface that is already flat and sound. It gives you a smooth, paint-ready wall. It does not fix a wall that is bowed, damp or crumbling — it just gives you a smooth version of the same problem.

A full re-plaster means removing what's there, applying a backing coat (bonding, browning or hardwall depending on the substrate), letting it set, then finishing. It's more expensive because it's genuinely more work, but it's the only correct answer when plaster has blown.

The knuckle test

Tap across the wall with your knuckle. Solid, dull sound means the plaster is bonded to the wall. A hollow, drummy sound means it has separated behind the surface, and skimming over it will only buy you a year or two before it cracks again.

The test is simple and you can do it yourself, and it tells you which of the two jobs you are actually buying. If you're finding cracks rather than hollow patches, the type of crack matters — hairline shrinkage cracks in fresh plaster are cosmetic, while stepped or widening cracks point to movement that needs investigating before anyone skims over it. There's a full breakdown in why plaster cracks and what each type means.

What a proper quote should include

Anything missing from this list should be queried before you accept:

  • Full surface area being covered, in m², with the ceiling shown separately
  • Whether the price is skim only or includes a backing coat
  • Removal and disposal of old plaster, and who is paying for the skip
  • Protection — dust sheets, masking, floor covering
  • Number of coats and the plaster product being used
  • Drying time before decorating can start
  • Whether making good around sockets, switches and coving is included
  • Waste removal at the end

Common extras that catch people out

Coving and cornice. Original cornice in older Southport and Burnley properties needs cutting in around by hand, and that is slow work. Expect £8–£15 per metre extra where existing cornice is being kept and worked around.

Corner beads and stop beads. On any job involving new plasterboard, beading is essential and is usually included — but confirm it.

Making good after first fix. If an electrician or plumber has chased the walls, someone has to fill those chases before the skim. It's not automatically included.

PVA and priming. New plasterboard needs sealing before the finish coat. Bare brick needs treating differently again. Any competent plasterer factors this in, but it should be visible in the quote.

How to get the price down without regretting it

  • Clear the room yourself. Furniture out, carpets lifted, curtains and rails down. That can save half an hour to an hour of chargeable time.
  • Do several rooms at once. Set-up, protection and material runs are largely fixed costs. Three rooms in one visit costs meaningfully less than three separate visits.
  • Book outside peak. January and February are quieter than May to September. Lead times shorten and pricing is often softer.
  • Don't skip the backing coat. The one thing never worth cutting. A skim over blown plaster is money you will spend twice.

Getting an accurate figure for your room

Anyone giving a firm price without seeing the walls is guessing, and the guess will be built with a safety margin you're paying for. A ten-minute look tells a plasterer whether it's a skim or a strip-out, and the difference between those two answers is several hundred pounds.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to plaster a room?

An average double bedroom, walls and ceiling, is typically one to two days of work. A straight skim on sound walls can be done in a day. A hack-off and full re-plaster usually takes two to three days including setting time between coats.

How long before I can paint new plaster?

Between seven and fourteen days depending on ventilation, room temperature and time of year. The plaster must go from dark brown to uniformly pale pink across the entire surface, with no dark patches remaining. Painting too early traps moisture and causes the paint to peel.

Is it cheaper to plaster or plasterboard?

Overboarding and skimming is usually cheaper than hacking off and re-plastering, because it removes the labour of stripping out and the cost of disposal. It does lose you 12mm of room dimension on each boarded surface, which matters in a small room.

Do I need to be at home while the work is done?

Not for the duration, but you should be there at the start and at the end of the first day. Plastering is dusty and disruptive and it's better to see how the room is being protected before the work is well underway.

Can you plaster over Artex?

Yes, provided it isn't heavily textured and is sound. Heavy texture is usually better overboarded. Artex applied before the mid-1980s may contain asbestos — it should be tested before any scraping or sanding, and that testing is not something to skip.
