Painting the outside of a house in the North West costs between £900 and £4,500 in 2026. A small terrace with modest frontage sits at the bottom. A detached property needing full scaffolding, render repairs and all woodwork sits at the top.

The spread is wide because exterior work has two costs most people don't anticipate: getting to the surface, and repairing what's underneath it. Here's how the numbers actually break down.

North West pricing runs roughly 20–30% under the South East for the same work. National averages published online are usually London-weighted and will make local quotes look suspiciously cheap.

The three things that actually drive the price

Access

This is the cost people forget. Anything above single-storey height needs a tower or scaffold, and on a two-storey semi that's frequently £650 to £1,200 before a brush is lifted. It's a real cost, it's charged at cost by any honest contractor, and it appears as a separate line on the quote.

Where scaffolding is going up anyway, it is always worth doing the fascias, soffits, guttering and upstairs windows in the same visit. Coming back in two years to paint the bargeboards means paying for the scaffold twice.

Terraced properties with rear access down a ginnel — very common in Burnley, Padiham, Nelson and Colne — sometimes can't take a scaffold at the back at all, which changes the method and the price.

Condition of the substrate

Painting is the last 30% of an exterior job. The rest is repair.

  • Sound render or pebbledash — wash down, treat any organic growth, fill hairlines, two coats. Straightforward.
  • Cracked render — cracks raked out, filled, and where hollow, cut out and patched. Adds £30–£60 per m² over the affected area.
  • Previously painted with the wrong product — plastic-heavy masonry paint on solid stone or brick traps moisture. Sometimes it has to come off, and that's a significant job in itself.
  • Flaking timber — old paint scraped back, bare timber primed, rot cut out and made good. Rotten window cills are the single most common exterior surprise.
  • Organic growth — green and black algae on north-facing walls. Needs a fungicidal wash and time to work before painting, not just a pressure wash.

Surface area, which is not the same as house size

A two-storey semi with a lot of glazing might have less painted wall area than a bungalow with large blank gables. Exterior work is priced per m² of actual coating surface, and the shape of the property matters as much as its footprint.

What should be in an exterior quote

Anything missing here is worth asking about before you sign:

  • Access method — scaffold, tower or ladder, and who supplies it
  • Preparation stated in detail — washing down, fungicidal treatment, scraping, filling, sanding
  • Repairs — render cracks, rotten timber, defective cills, and whether these are included or provisional
  • Priming and stabilising — bare masonry needs a stabilising solution; bare timber needs primer
  • Product and number of coats — two full coats of masonry paint is standard, three on a colour change
  • Rainwater goods — whether guttering and downpipes are included or extra
  • Weather contingency — what happens if it rains for a week
  • Guarantee — a good exterior system should give eight to ten years on masonry

Product choice and why it changes the price

Standard masonry paint — around £45–£70 for 10 litres trade. Covers roughly 6–8m² per litre over two coats. Good for five to eight years.

Premium flexible masonry — £90–£140 for 10 litres. Bridges hairline cracks and moves with the substrate. Worth it on render that has a history of cracking.

Breathable mineral paint — £120–£180 for 10 litres. Essential on solid stone and older brick where the wall needs to release moisture. Common requirement on the pre-1919 stock across East Lancashire and on older Southport properties. It costs more and it's the right answer where it applies.

Using a cheap plastic masonry paint on a solid stone wall is the classic false economy: it traps moisture in the wall, and within a few years it blisters off, taking the face of the stone with it.

Coastal properties are a different job

Salt-laden air on the Sefton coast — Southport, Birkdale, Ainsdale, Formby — attacks exterior coatings considerably faster than inland conditions. Metalwork corrodes quicker, timber degrades faster, and standard masonry paint doesn't last as long as it does in Burnley.

5–7 years

Realistic repaint cycle on the Sefton coast, against eight to ten years inland — and a stronger case for spending more on the coating up front.

Where the money is best spent

If the budget doesn't stretch to everything at once:

  1. Timber first. Bare or flaking timber is actively deteriorating. Masonry with tired paint is just tired. Wood rots, walls don't.
  2. Fascias, soffits and bargeboards next. They're the highest exposure and the most expensive to replace.
  3. Masonry last. It looks the most dramatic, which is why people want it first, but it's the least urgent.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should a house exterior be repainted?

Masonry typically lasts eight to ten years inland, five to seven on the coast. Timber needs attention every four to six years, sooner on south and west-facing elevations that take the weather and the sun.

Is scaffolding always necessary?

Not always. Single-storey work and low bungalows can often be done from ladders or a tower. Two-storey work above a conservatory, a bay or a sloping garden usually requires a scaffold, both for safety and because you cannot do a proper job hanging off a ladder.

Can you paint a house exterior in one day?

A front elevation on a small terrace, possibly. Anything larger is three to eight days including preparation and drying time between coats. Anyone offering a full exterior in a day is not preparing it.

Does exterior paint need two coats?

Yes. One coat over existing paint of the same colour might look acceptable on the day, but the film thickness is insufficient and it will fail early. Two coats is the minimum and a colour change often needs three.

What happens if it rains during the job?

Work stops. Masonry paint applied to a damp surface, or rained on before it has formed a film, will fail. A realistic quote builds weather delay into the programme rather than pretending it won't happen.

Should I pressure wash the walls myself first?

Please don't. A pressure washer drives water into render cracks and behind coatings, and the wall then needs weeks to dry out before it can be painted. Washing down is part of the job and is done at the right pressure with the right product.
